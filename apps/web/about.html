Method

A robot reads a PDF. You read the robot. Here's everything worth knowing about the bit in between.

The pipeline, in four lines
  1. Every few hours a job fetches each distributor's page and hashes what it finds.
  2. Changed? Archive the exact bytes forever, then parse the table.
  3. A validator rejects anything malformed and keeps the last good version rather than publishing junk.
  4. The result is a static JSON file. Your browser does the rest. No server, no database.
Why you'll never see a percentage
Any score can be printed as a percentage. That doesn't make it a probability. Earning one needs a dataset of verified address-to-feeder pairs, which nobody publishes. So confidence is capped at Medium and no number is shown. The cap lifts by itself the day that dataset exists. The honest behaviour is the default, and relaxing it takes evidence.
Where the map is guessing
No distributor publishes service boundaries, and OpenStreetMap has no Dhaka North/South city corporation limits either. So the DESCO/DPDC line is constructed: we geocode the zone names each utility prints on its own sheets and split the city by whichever is nearest. Reliable in the middle of a territory, a coin-toss along the join, which is why those borders are dashed. Outside Dhaka it's coarser still: administrative divisions standing in for service areas, which ignores that rural supply belongs to BREB.
Things we know are wrong
  • DESCO publishes one sheet per weekday. If the newest we can fetch is Sunday's and today is Wednesday, the page says so rather than pretending.
  • Feeder area descriptions are free text, inconsistently spelled, and often overlap.
  • DESCO's own PDF has rows printed on top of each other. One reads MGuolhsahkahnali:, which is "Gulshan" interleaved with "Mohakhali". Those rows are excluded, not guessed.
  • DPDC mixes digital sheets with scanned ones. All 36 zones are read: 19 digital PDFs parsed directly, and 17 scanned photographs read by finding the ruled grid and measuring which cells are shaded. The Bengali hour labels on those scans defeat every free OCR we tried, so a Bangla reader read each sheet's hour layout once and it is recorded in the repository. The layout is checked against the image on every run: if a sheet's column count stops matching, the parser refuses rather than shifting the hours.
  • NESCO publishes photographs of paper.
  • An outage you experience may just be a local fault. It looks identical from your side of the meter.
What we collect about you
Nothing. No account, no cookie, no analytics, no server of ours in the loop. Place search runs against a list bundled with the page, so typing doesn't phone anyone. Saved locations live in your browser and vanish when you clear site data. "Copy link" puts coordinates in the part of a URL browsers never send to servers.

We never ask for your bill, account or meter number, and you should never enter one here. Those belong only on your distributor's own site.
What it costs to run
Nothing, on purpose. A project that needs money to stay alive eventually stops being free. Static hosting, scheduled jobs, map tiles, geocoding and boundaries all come from free tiers that never asked for a card. There's no database bill because the published JSON files are the database.
